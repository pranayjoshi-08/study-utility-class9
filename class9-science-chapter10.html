📖 Chapter Overview

Sound is a form of energy produced by vibrating objects. It travels through a medium as a wave.

In this chapter, we study how sound is produced, how it travels, its characteristics and some important applications.

🎵 Production of Sound

Sound is produced by vibrating objects.

When an object vibrates, it causes nearby particles of the medium to vibrate. These vibrations travel through the medium and reach our ears.

Examples

  • 🎸 A vibrating guitar string produces sound.
  • 🥁 A vibrating drum membrane produces sound.
  • 🎤 Vocal cords vibrate to produce human voice.

🌊 Sound Needs a Medium

Sound needs a material medium such as a solid, liquid or gas to travel.

Sound cannot travel through a vacuum because there are no particles to transmit the vibrations.

↔️ Longitudinal Waves

Sound waves in air are longitudinal waves. In a longitudinal wave, particles of the medium vibrate parallel to the direction in which the wave travels.

Compressions

Regions where particles are closer together are called compressions.

Rarefactions

Regions where particles are farther apart are called rarefactions.

📏 Wavelength

Wavelength is the distance between two successive compressions or two successive rarefactions.

It is represented by λ and its SI unit is metre (m).

🔄 Frequency

Frequency is the number of complete vibrations or oscillations made per second.

Its SI unit is hertz (Hz).

1 Hz = 1 vibration per second

⏱️ Time Period

The time taken to complete one vibration is called the time period.

It is represented by T.

T = 1/f

🚀 Speed of Sound

The speed of sound depends on the properties of the medium through which it travels.

Sound generally travels fastest in solids, slower in liquids and slowest in gases.

The speed of a wave is related to frequency and wavelength:

v = fλ

📢 Amplitude

Amplitude is the maximum displacement of a vibrating particle from its mean position.

Greater amplitude generally produces a louder sound.

🎶 Pitch

Pitch tells us how high or low a sound appears to be.

Pitch depends mainly on the frequency of vibration.

  • Higher frequency → higher pitch
  • Lower frequency → lower pitch

🔊 Loudness

Loudness is related to the amplitude of vibration and describes how strong or soft a sound appears to our ears.

Greater amplitude generally means greater loudness.

👂 Human Hearing

The human ear detects sound vibrations and converts them into signals that are interpreted by the brain.

For a typical healthy human ear, the approximate audible frequency range is 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz.

🐘 Infrasonic and Ultrasonic Sounds

Infrasonic Sound

Sounds with frequencies below 20 Hz are called infrasonic sounds.

Ultrasonic Sound

Sounds with frequencies above 20,000 Hz are called ultrasonic sounds.

🔁 Reflection of Sound

Sound can be reflected when it strikes a suitable surface.

Hard and smooth surfaces generally reflect sound better than soft surfaces.

🏛️ Echo

An echo is the repetition of a sound heard after the reflected sound reaches the listener.

A sufficiently large time gap between the original and reflected sound is needed for the two sounds to be heard separately.

📣 Reverberation

Reverberation is the persistence of sound in an enclosed space due to repeated reflections from surfaces.

Excessive reverberation can make speech difficult to understand.

🩺 Applications of Ultrasound

Ultrasound has many useful applications because of its high frequency.

  • Medical imaging and diagnosis.
  • Detecting internal structures.
  • Industrial testing of materials.
  • Cleaning small objects and equipment.

🚢 SONAR

SONAR uses sound waves to detect objects and measure distances underwater.

It works using the reflection of sound waves from objects or surfaces.
